What is it

Recall Is the Hardest Part of Memory

We already know memory has three stages: encoding, storage, and retrieval. Issue 04 is all about that third stage — retrieval — the act of pulling a stored memory back into conscious awareness. This is what happens when you sit down for an exam and try to remember what you studied.

Think of neural pathways like footpaths in a field. The more you walk them, the clearer they become. But your biology also matters — the condition of your brain on any given day directly affects how quickly and accurately you can retrieve information.

Recall is not automatic. It depends on how strong those neural pathways are. And the condition of your brain on any given day — shaped heavily by your sugar intake, hydration, and physical activity — has a direct effect on retrieval speed and accuracy.

Why You Should Care

Three Things You Do Every Day Are Affecting Your Recall Right Now

🍚

Sugar

Small, steady amounts of glucose from complex carbohydrates keep the hippocampus functioning well. High-sugar spikes from sodas or candy cause a rapid crash that impairs the prefrontal cortex and makes it harder to retrieve memories under pressure. Research from UCLA found that a high-fructose diet over time actually slows brain signalling and weakens recall.

💧

Water

Your brain is approximately 75% water. Even mild dehydration — as little as 1 to 2% of body weight — reduces short-term memory, slows reaction time, and impairs concentration. When you are dehydrated, neurons fire less efficiently, making it harder to access stored information quickly. Students who drank water before cognitive tests consistently scored higher.

🏃

Exercise

Physical activity increases blood flow to the brain and triggers the release of BDNF — Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor — a protein that strengthens existing neural connections and grows new ones. Even a 20-minute brisk walk before studying has been shown to improve memory consolidation and retrieval. Exercise also reduces cortisol, a major blocker of clear thinking.

Putting it together

Sugar, water, and exercise are not just health habits. They are memory tools.

Teens who maintain steady blood sugar, stay hydrated, and move regularly are giving their brains the biological conditions needed to encode and recall information at a higher level.

These are choices you make before you even open a textbook.

Cool Fact · Mind = Blown

Exercise Grows New Brain Cells in the Memory Centre

For a long time, scientists believed you were born with a fixed number of brain cells and that was it. We now know that is not true.

The hippocampus is one of the only regions in the brain that can generate brand new neurons throughout your life — a process called neurogenesis. And the number one trigger for this? Aerobic exercise. Running, dancing, swimming, and even fast walking all stimulate the growth of new hippocampal cells, directly boosting your capacity to form and retrieve memories.
Myth Buster

Let's Set the Record Straight

✕ The Myth

"Eating sugar before an exam gives your brain a boost." Many teens reach for energy drinks, sweets, or biscuits right before a test, believing the sugar will sharpen their thinking.

✓ The Truth

A sugar spike causes a rapid rise and then a crash in blood glucose. During that crash, focus and recall both drop. What your brain actually needs before an exam is steady fuel: complex carbs, some protein, and water. Save the sweets for after the exam.

Try This Today

The Recall-Ready Morning Routine

On your next exam day or heavy study day, try this routine in the morning. It is designed to prime your brain for recall using everything covered in this issue.

1
Drink a full glass of water before anything else. Your brain has been without water for 7 or more hours. Rehydrate before you pick up your phone or your notes.
2
Eat a brain-friendly breakfast. Try eggs with whole grain bread, oats with fruit, or rice and beans. Avoid high-sugar cereals or pastries that will cause a mid-morning crash.
3
Move for 15 to 20 minutes. A brisk walk, a quick dance session, or light stretching. You do not need a gym. This raises BDNF and increases blood flow to your hippocampus before the work begins.
4
Keep water nearby while studying. Aim to drink consistently throughout your session, not just when you feel thirsty. Thirst is already a sign of mild dehydration.
5
Test yourself — do not just re-read. Use retrieval practice from Issue 03. Close the book, write what you remember, check the gaps. Your primed brain will surprise you.
💡

Why it works: Each step targets a specific biological condition for recall. Water restores neural efficiency. A steady-fuel breakfast keeps blood glucose stable. Exercise raises BDNF and reduces cortisol. Together, they create the optimal brain environment for memory retrieval. This is not motivation advice — it is applied neuroscience.
