In the United States, racial and ethnic disparities in mental health service usage persist across decades. Understanding why they exist is the first step to dismantling them.

Health disparities among racial groups in the United States have been documented across a wide range of categories — disease prevention, treatment of congenital conditions, injuries, and more. Among these, mental health stands out as a persistent and under-examined point of inequality. Because this continues to be an issue, it is important to understand both the scale of these disparities and their causes, in order to address them meaningfully.

01

Trends in Usage of Mental Health Services

From 2008 to 2012, adults with any mental health condition were tracked across racial and ethnic groups for their use of mental health services. White adults used these services at a rate of 46.3%. By contrast, Black or African-American adults used them at 29.8%, Hispanic adults at 27.3%, and Asian adults at just 18.1%.

By 2020, usage had increased across all groups — yet the gaps had not meaningfully closed. White adults were at 52%, while Asian adults remained at 20%.

Though the overall trajectory is upward, the proportional gaps have remained stubbornly consistent over more than a decade. These results raise a critical question: why do these disparities continue to persist, even as awareness of mental health has broadly increased?

02

Socioeconomic Inequities

Socioeconomic status is one of the most significant drivers of unequal access to mental healthcare. Access to treatment often depends on access to insurance — and insurance access is closely tied to income and employment.

60%
of people who are uninsured in the US are people of color. More than 80% of all uninsured individuals belong to low-income families — and without coverage, many cannot afford mental health treatment.

Recent policy changes compound this further. Cutbacks to Medicaid and the ACA Marketplace are projected to leave over 14 million more people uninsured by 2034. The populations most likely to be affected are the same ones already facing the largest gaps in mental health service usage.

03

Cultural Stigma

Even when access to care exists, many individuals hesitate to seek it. In numerous communities, openly expressing mental health struggles is treated as taboo — a sign of personal weakness rather than a medical need. This makes conversations about mental health genuinely difficult to have, and help genuinely difficult to ask for.

Representation in the profession itself is part of the problem. Only around 14% of all psychologists in the United States are people of color — a figure that breaks down further when divided by specific groups. When patients cannot see themselves reflected in the people providing care, feelings of distrust and dissatisfaction can make an already difficult decision even harder.

04

Representation in Healthcare

Underrepresentation of minorities in mental health professions does not only affect patient trust — it can also affect the quality of care received. Healthcare providers, regardless of their own background, may carry implicit biases that shape how they engage with patients from different groups, often without being aware of those biases at all.

In a field with such low minority representation, racial biases — implicit or otherwise — have the potential to affect a disproportionately large share of the care being delivered.

This is not a problem that can be resolved through individual effort alone. It requires systemic changes to who enters mental health professions, how providers are trained, and what structures exist to hold care quality accountable across patient populations.

A path forward

What Can Be Done?

1

Access to quality healthcare is a right, not a privilege. Advocating for and supporting policies that expand access — particularly for low-income and uninsured populations — is one of the most direct levers available.

2

With diversity comes strength. Supporting initiatives that increase minority representation in mental health professions improves both patient trust and care quality across the board.

By voting for and advocating for policies built on these ideas, we move toward a system where mental healthcare is not only accessible — but where every person can receive it from a provider who understands their experience.
